Introduction

Pakistan's urban population has grown from 51 million in 2000 to 96 million in 2025,[1] representing one of the most rapid urban transitions in South Asia. Over the same period, adult obesity rates skyrocketed from 4.5% to 31% — a staggering 6.9× increase.[3]

Fast-food consumption among young Pakistanis has increased significantly, contributing to major shifts in dietary behavior within urban environments.[2] Rising rates of coronary artery disease (CAD) and other noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) have prompted growing concern regarding the relationship between urbanization, dietary transition, and population health outcomes.[3][4]

Key Research Question
How has urbanization in Pakistan shaped cardiometabolic health outcomes such as obesity and cardiovascular disease?
2
Background — Urban–Rural Health Divergence
Lit. Review · Part I

A 2023 JPTCP study found CAD prevalence of 37.2% in urban versus 28.5% in rural Pakistani adults — an 8.7 percentage point gap. The steepest divergence was observed in the 40–60 age cohort, likely due to sedentary employment, chronic stress, and proximity to fast-food environments.

Fast food's rapid urban expansion in Pakistan is driven by aggressive marketing strategies, newfound affordability, and convenience. Its concentration in cities — Karachi, Lahore, Islamabad, and Faisalabad — has propelled fast food to the second-largest food sector nationally, capturing 27% of the market.[2]

3
Background — Household Food Spending
Lit. Review · Part II

The PBS Household Integrated Economic Survey 2024–25 found that Pakistanis spend, on average, approximately 44% of household income on food and drink products (Restaurants & Hotels + Food & Non-Alcoholic Beverages + Beverages & Drinks).[6]

This elevated expenditure share reflects the growing accessibility of processed and fast food products, as well as the dietary priorities of an urbanizing middle class navigating competing economic pressures.

4
Background — Western Food Adoption
Lit. Review · Part III

Between 2000 and 2023, the value of food imports as a percentage of total merchandise exports rose from 13% to 30%.[2] This metric — commonly used in macroeconomics — reflects a growing reliance on imported food commodities, signaling a fundamental shift in dietary composition.

Expenditure on imported food items surged from $49.9 million in 2017 to $75 million in 2018, representing a 67% increase in a single year — underscoring accelerating consumer preference for Western food products.[7]
